# The writing checks

What the colours in the editor mean, what each check looks for, and how
to use them without letting them write the chapter for you.

## How it looks

The checks work the way the Hemingway editor does. Beside the text is a
panel: the **reading grade** in large type, the number of words and
sentences and how long the chapter takes to read, and then one coloured
card for each kind of trouble -- *1 of 9 sentences is very hard to read*,
*4 adverbs. Aim for 1 or fewer.* Each card is the same colour as its marks
in the text, so the panel is also the key to them.

@figure checks-panel.png | The panel beside the text: the grade, then a card per check, then the craft checks.

In the text itself:

- **Red** -- a sentence that is very hard to read. **Yellow** -- hard.
- **Blue** -- an adverb. **Green** -- passive voice. **Purple** -- a word or
  phrase with a simpler alternative.
- **Wavy underline** -- a possible misspelling.
- **Plain underline**, in a colour of its own -- one of the craft checks
  (filler words, repeated words, filter verbs, dialogue tags, repeated
  openings). They are a second, quieter layer, because they are more often
  a matter of taste.

The fills are solid, so an adverb inside a hard sentence is a blue block
on a yellow one rather than a muddy mix of the two.

## Using it

- **Hover over a mark** to see what it is and, where there is one, what
  it proposes.
- **Click a mark** to open the same note with buttons: a proposed word or
  rewrite goes into the text with one click, and a misspelt name can be
  added to the story's dictionary from there.
- **Click a card** to hide or show that kind of mark. The card keeps
  counting while its marks are hidden, and says *hidden*, so you know
  what you have put away.
- **Write** and **Revise**, at the top of the panel: Write takes every mark
  out of the text while you draft; Revise brings them back. Hemingway has
  the same pair, for the same reason -- a first draft and a second pass are
  different jobs.
- **Checks as a list**, above the text, turns every mark into a list you
  can go through in order, each item selecting its words. It is for a
  screen reader, and for anyone who prefers a to-do list to a colouring
  book.

The marks are drawn behind the words as you write; **Preview** shows the
chapter clean, as a reader will see it.
On a phone the panel folds into one line above the text -- *Grade 5 · 18
flagged* -- that opens with a tap.

Nothing is sent anywhere: the checks run in your browser, and none of them
is AI. They are rules, and a rule does not know what you meant.

## What each check looks for

**Hard and very hard sentences.** Long sentences, and sentences built out
of long words, scored the way a reading grade is. Over about twenty words,
or at the level of a dense textbook, a sentence is yellow; over forty, or
far past that level, red. A long sentence can be exactly right -- a rush,
a list, a breathless moment -- but a paragraph of them is usually a
paragraph that needs splitting.

**Adverbs.** Words ending in *-ly* that lean on a verb: *walked slowly*,
*said quietly*. Often a stronger verb says it in one word, and where
there is an obvious one the check offers it: *walked slowly* → *ambled*.
It also catches the adverb that only repeats its verb (*hurried quickly*).

**Passive voice.** *The report was written by the night crew.* Passive
hides who did the thing, which is sometimes the point and often an
accident. Where the sentence says who did it, the check can turn it round
for you: *The night crew wrote the report.*

**Simpler alternatives.** Words with a plainer twin: *utilize* → *use*,
*commence* → *begin*. In a character's mouth the long word may be the
character; in the narration it is usually just longer.

**Spelling.** A real dictionary. It does not know the names you invented;
add them to the **story dictionary** from the mark itself or on the story
page, and names you put in the story bible are added for you.

**Filler words** (craft). *Very*, *really*, *just*, *actually*,
*basically*, and phrases like *due to the fact that*. Try the sentence
without it; if it still works, it did not need it.

**Repeated words** (craft). The same noticeable word twice within about
fifty words -- *the anchorage ... the anchorage*. Short and common words
are left alone. Repetition on purpose is a device; by accident it is an
echo the reader hears and you did not.

**Filter verbs** (craft). *She saw the door open. He felt the cold.* A
verb of seeing or feeling puts the reader one step outside the scene;
often the thing itself is stronger: *the door opened*. Only flagged straight
after a subject, and never inside dialogue.

**Dialogue tags** (craft). Speech verbs that ask to be noticed --
*exclaimed*, *hissed*, *retorted* -- next to a line of dialogue, and
adverbs propping up *said* (*said angrily*). *Said* and *asked* disappear
on the page, which is what a tag is for. Keep the loud one when the sound
is the point.

**Repeated openings** (craft). Three sentences in a row starting with the
same word (*She saw ... She saw ... She saw*), or two in a row starting
with an *-ing* word. It reads as a stutter, and it is almost impossible to
notice while writing the sentences one at a time.

## The reading grade

The American school year that could follow the text on a first read,
worked out from sentence length and syllables per word (the
Flesch-Kincaid formula). The word beside it is a rough verdict: *Very easy*
up to 6, *Good* up to 9, *OK* up to 12, *Hard* beyond. Most published
fiction lands between 4 and 8; children's books lower, a dense literary
chapter higher.

There is no grade that is wrong. A hard chapter written on purpose is a
hard chapter written on purpose. The grade is useful for noticing that a
scene has drifted somewhere you did not mean it to go, and useless as a
target.

## The targets

The cards say *aim for N or fewer* for adverbs and passive voice. Those
numbers are this site's own -- about one adverb in a hundred words, about
one passive sentence in five -- because Hemingway does not publish its
own. They are a nudge, not a rule. A chapter can go over both and be
better for it.

## On the chapter page

The author also has the same panel on the chapter page, folded into one
line above the text: *Readability · Grade 5 · 18 flagged*. It is there in
**Revise**, the third position of the switch under the title (Read,
Review, Revise), which marks the text as the editor does; Read and Review
leave the text alone. Open the line for the cards.
