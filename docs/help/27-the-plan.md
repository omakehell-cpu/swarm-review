# The plan: arcs, and chapters before they are written

Lay a story out before you write it -- the arcs it is built from, and the
chapters still to come -- then fill it in, in whatever order the writing
comes.

**Plan**, on the story page and beside *Outline*, is for the people who
write the story. Nobody reading it sees the plan or anything in it: a
chapter you have planned is not a chapter until you write it.

## Planning a chapter

**Plan a chapter** takes a working title, what happens in it, and where it
goes -- at the very start, or after any chapter, written or planned. It
shows in the plan as *Planned*, in italics, between the chapters it sits
between. The arrows move it up and down; past another planned chapter it
swaps places, past a written one it moves across it. (Written chapters are
moved on the [outline](/help/writing-a-chapter), as always.)

**Plan several at once**, under the form, takes a whole outline pasted in
-- one chapter a line, with the lines under it that start with a dash or a
space as its notes -- and plans them all, in order:

```
The storm comes
  - the relay goes down
  - Pell goes out alone
Night watch
The long repair
```

**Write it** opens a new chapter with the working title as its title, and
the plan's notes in a **Plan** tab beside the text, with the arcs it is in
and what each of them is for. The notes are not the chapter's summary:
they are yours, and readers never see them.

Write in whatever order the writing comes. **Save draft** keeps what you
have written on the plan, **for you alone** -- the plan says *Your draft*,
how long it is and when you saved it, and **Go on writing** takes you back
to it. The other people who write the story see that you are writing it,
not what, and cannot move it or delete it until you publish it.

**Publish chapter** makes it a chapter: it leaves the plan and takes its
place in the story -- where the plan has it at that moment, even if other
chapters were written since -- and any arc that started or ended on it
now starts or ends on the chapter. The plan's notes stay with it: in the
plan, under *What the plan said*, and in its editor's **Plan** tab. Write
the ending first if that is the one you can see.

## Arcs, and arcs inside arcs

An arc is a stretch of the story with a shape: something happens, and
something is different at the end of it. **Add an arc** asks for a name,
where it starts and ends, and three questions -- because an empty box asks
you to be inspired, and a question asks you to answer it:

- **What happens in it** -- the events, in a few lines.
- **What is different at the end** -- who or what is not the same when it
  is over. If nothing is, it may not be an arc.
- **Why it matters to the whole story** -- what the story would lose
  without it.

An arc that has not answered them yet says so, in the plan, with a link to
answer them. An arc six chapters long or more with no smaller arcs inside
it asks whether there is a turn halfway through, with a link to add one.

At the top of the plan, **the arcs over the chapters** draw it at a
glance: the chapters in reading order, a dotted square for a planned one,
and above them a bar for each arc across the chapters it covers, a row
for each level of nesting.

Arcs nest. A **top-level arc** is a book or a part: it runs until the next
one starts, and its name is the heading that opens it in the compiled
story -- the same thing as the *Arc* field in a chapter's details, which
still works and stays in step with the plan. So a top-level arc is not
asked where it ends. An arc **inside** another has its own end, and can have smaller arcs inside it in turn, as deep as the
story is built.

An arc can start or end on a planned chapter, so the shape can be there
before the words are. Each one says how many of its chapters are written,
how many are planned, and how long it is so far.

If an arc does not fit -- it ends before it starts, runs outside the arc it
is in, or overlaps the one before it -- the plan says so under its name
rather than guessing what you meant. On a chapter page, the people who
write the story see which arcs it is in, under the title.

## Taking things out

Deleting a planned chapter takes its notes, and your draft of it, with
it. An arc that started on
it now starts on the next thing, and one that ended on it ends on the one
before.

Deleting an arc never touches a chapter. The smaller arcs inside it are
kept: inside a bigger arc they move up into it, and at the top level they
go into the arc before, which now runs on over their chapters.
